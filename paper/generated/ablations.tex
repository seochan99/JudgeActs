The position bias could be an artifact of our interface rather than of the judge. We ran three post-hoc ablations with Qwen on all 300 prompts, leaving the frozen main results unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:ablations}, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ablations}).

\textit{Output format.} The main contract asks for a full ranking whose first entry is the choice, which could prime the first label. With a choice-only contract, Qwen still picks the first slot in 44.8\% of calls (uniform 27.8\%) and changes its choice across orders on 54.0\% of prompts. Its gain over random is 0.047 [0.024, 0.069], and unanimity keeps 46.0\% of prompts with gain 0.123 [0.094, 0.153].

\textit{Label identity.} Replacing A/B/C/D with opaque two-character codes unrelated to order, Qwen picks the first-presented image in 51.8\% of calls (uniform 27.8\%) and changes its choice on 62.7\% of prompts, with gain 0.021 [$-$0.001, 0.044].

\textit{Full rotation.} Adding a fourth rotation for the 200 four-image pools places every image in every slot once. Slot shares are then A 52.5\%, B 20.4\%, C 14.4\%, D 12.8\% (uniform 25.0\%). Unanimity over four orders keeps 27.5\% of these pools with regret 0.095 and gain 0.127 [0.073, 0.178], and voting over the four orders gives gain 0.061 [0.034, 0.087].

In every variant the bias toward the first slot remains and unanimity still keeps prompts on which the judge beats random. The preference is a property of the judge, not of the letter labels or the ranking format.

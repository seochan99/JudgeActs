\section{Introduction}
A vision-language model (VLM) that selects one of several generated images does more than report a quality score. Its choice determines which candidate a user would receive. The practical question is whether that choice improves the available outputs, not just whether the judge's scores correlate with human ratings.

For culturally situated requests, visual polish and recognizable symbols can be poor guides to prompt satisfaction. An image may include a requested object while misrepresenting the social context around it. A selector can favor that image even when another candidate better satisfies the request. Evaluating this choice requires ratings that did not come from the selector itself.

CulturalFrames provides generated images and human ratings that distinguish missing explicit expectations, missing implicit expectations, and stereotypes. It also reports weak agreement between automatic metrics and human judgments \citep{culturalframes}. We use the public CulturalFrames release to study a different decision: choosing which existing image to return.

For each prompt, an open-weight VLM sees three or four candidate images and selects one (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}), without generator names, country metadata, or ratings. We compare the selected image's released ratings with exact random choice and with the best candidate in the same fixed pool, so differences between policies arise from selection rather than generation.

The primary outcome is \emph{selection regret}, measured from the mean human prompt-alignment rating. It is the gap between the highest-rated available candidate and the selected image. Prompt alignment measures request satisfaction, not cultural accuracy alone. We therefore report stereotype and missing-expectation ratings separately. We also measure how often the selected score falls below random expected utility. We call this the \emph{below-mean rate} (BMR).

We make three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An audit of judges as decision-makers.} We score each returned image against held-out human ratings and against random choice from the same candidates, so every comparison is paired and free of the judge's own scores.
\item \textbf{Evidence of strong position bias in small VLM judges.} Both judges favor early positions, and the same prompt can yield the worst or the best available image depending only on candidate order (Figures~\ref{fig:hero} and~\ref{fig:teaser}). Ablations show that the bias is not caused by our output format or label letters.
\item \textbf{An audit of agreement gates.} Keeping only decisions that are consistent across orders retains choices that beat random, whereas agreement with a weaker second judge does not. A gate must therefore be audited on the prompts it rejects as well as on those it keeps.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Cultural representation and evaluation.}
CulturalFrames supplies culturally situated prompts, multiple generated images, and ratings of missing expectations and stereotypes \citep{culturalframes}. Its analysis compares automatic metrics with human ratings of individual images. Our analysis instead evaluates choices within a candidate set. \citet{seo2025} study cultural representation in image generation and editing; we study selection among existing outputs.

\citet{chang2026} train a cultural reward model on preferences derived from CulturalFrames and evaluate pairwise agreement. This is closely related evidence that cultural image preferences can be learned. We do not propose a competing reward model; we audit frozen, prompted selectors and the requests retained by their agreement gates.

\paragraph{VLM judges.}
\citet{zou2026} show that multimodal judges can favor informative answers without adequately using visual evidence. \citet{park2026} examine conflicts between textual narratives and perception, and train judges to ground their decisions more reliably. Both concern tasks distinct from our candidate-image selection setting. They motivate checking selected outcomes against independent ratings, but do not establish the mechanism behind the errors measured here.

K-Sort Eval uses multi-image VLM rankings, posterior correction, and adaptive comparisons to evaluate visual generators \citep{ksort2026}. Our study asks about the selected image within each prompt's candidate pool, rather than the accuracy or efficiency of a generator leaderboard. We do not claim that comparative image judging or best-of-pool selection is new.

\paragraph{Position bias in model judges.}
Language-model judges are sensitive to the order in which options are presented: pairwise judges favor a position \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair,shi2025judging}, and multiple-choice accuracy shifts with option order \citep{pezeshkpour2024order}. Multimodal judges also show position and consistency effects on vision-language benchmarks \citep{chen2024mllmjudge}, over-score earlier sentences and prefer their own captions when assessing image-text alignment \citep{saito2025haldec}, and can be swayed by visual manipulations unrelated to quality \citep{hwang2025fooling}. Proposed mitigations include swapping or averaging over orders \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair} and de-biased judging protocols \citep{asaria2026judging}. VLM evaluators are also used to audit generated images at scale, with human validation on a subset \citep{seo2026portrait}. We measure the same phenomenon when the judge's output is an action, and ask how it interacts with agreement-based abstention.

\paragraph{Judgments used as decision rules.}
\citet{yao2026} investigate multimodal reinforcement learning with imperfect rewards, where gains on a proxy can diverge from task performance. WORLDVIEW examines cultural stereotyping introduced during prompt revision \citep{urman2026}. We isolate a later decision, selecting an existing candidate. The experiment does not optimize generators against a reward model or simulate a sequential feedback process.

\paragraph{Selective prediction.}
Risk--coverage analysis evaluates prediction with a reject option \citep{elyaniv2010,geifman2017}. We apply it to image selection, using agreement across orders or judges as gates. These gates yield measured operating points, not calibrated confidence scores or risk guarantees.

\section{Problem and Measures}
\label{sec:measures}
\paragraph{Selection task.} Let $p$ denote a prompt and $C_p=\{x_{p1},\ldots,x_{pm_p}\}$ its pool of $m_p\in\{3,4\}$ candidate images, already generated and held fixed. A \emph{selection policy} $\pi$ receives the prompt and the images, shown in a given order with labels, and either returns the index $\pi(p)\in\{1,\ldots,m_p\}$ of one candidate or abstains. We always record the chosen image's identity, not its label, so the same image can carry the label ``A'' in one order and ``C'' in another.

\paragraph{Human reference utility.} Each candidate $x_{pi}$ has released prompt-alignment ratings from several annotators, each 0, 0.5, or 1. Its utility $h_{pi}\in[0,1]$ is the mean of these ratings. Judges never see these ratings; they are joined to the chosen image only after selection.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Choosing uniformly at random has expected utility $\bar h_p=\frac{1}{m_p}\sum_{i=1}^{m_p} h_{pi}$, which we compute exactly rather than by simulation. The \emph{annotation oracle} picks the highest-rated candidate, with utility $h^\star_p=\max_i h_{pi}$; it is a reference point, not a deployable selector.

\paragraph{Outcome measures.} For a policy that acts on prompt $p$ and returns image $x_{p,\pi(p)}$, with utility $h_{p,\pi(p)}$, we report three quantities:
\begin{align}
\text{regret:}\quad & R_p(\pi)=h^\star_p-h_{p,\pi(p)}, \label{eq:regret}\\
\text{gain over random:}\quad & G_p(\pi)=h_{p,\pi(p)}-\bar h_p, \label{eq:gain}\\
\text{below-mean event:}\quad & B_p(\pi)=\mathbf{1}\{h_{p,\pi(p)}<\bar h_p\}. \label{eq:bmr}
\end{align}
Regret is the primary outcome: zero means the judge returned a best-rated image. Gain is positive when the judge beats random choice on the same pool, and the two are linked by $G_p=(h^\star_p-\bar h_p)-R_p$. Averaging $B_p$ over prompts gives the \emph{below-mean rate} (BMR), a score shortfall relative to random, not a measure of user harm. Random choice has its own below-mean probability, the share of candidates rated below $\bar h_p$, which we use as its matched comparison. We also report how often the policy returns a best-rated image, counting ties. Every average is taken over the set $A_\pi$ of prompts on which the policy acts, and each policy is compared with random choice on exactly those prompts.

\paragraph{Cultural-error outcomes.} CulturalFrames also releases, per image, the share of annotators who flag a stereotype, a missing explicit expectation, or a missing implicit expectation. For each of these error rates we report the chosen image's value minus the random-choice average over the same pool, so that negative values mean fewer errors than chance. These outcomes are kept separate from alignment and are not combined into a composite score. A pool is \emph{sensitive} to an error type when that error rate differs across its candidates, so that selection can change it; we report results on each sensitive subset (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). As a secondary utility we also use overall satisfaction, rescaled from its 1--5 scale to $[0,1]$.

\paragraph{Presentation orders and agreement gates.} Each pool is shown in $K=3$ cyclic orders $k\in\{1,2,3\}$ (order~1 is canonical, orders~2 and~3 rotate it). An order places the candidates in labeled positions, which we call slots A, B, C, and D; a preference for particular slots regardless of content is \emph{position bias}. Let $\pi_k(p)$ be the index chosen under order $k$, and let $q_p$ be the share of the three orders that agree on the most frequent choice ($1/3$, $2/3$, or $1$). A \emph{gate} with threshold $\tau$ acts only when $q_p\ge\tau$ and then returns that majority choice: $\tau=1$ is \emph{unanimity}, $\tau=2/3$ requires two of three, and $\tau=1/3$ always acts. The \emph{cross-model gate} acts only when two judges make the same order-1 choice. For every gate we report coverage $|A_\pi|/N$ over the $N=300$ prompts, the outcomes on the prompts it keeps, and, because a gate can look good simply by keeping easy pools, the same outcomes for random choice and for the ungated judge on both the kept and the rejected prompts.

\paragraph{Country groups.} Each prompt belongs to one of ten country contexts; we report group means of regret and gain with their random baselines, and \emph{worst-group regret}, the largest group mean. Country labels identify dataset contexts, not homogeneous cultural preferences.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Dataset and Frozen Splits}
We use the independently released CulturalFrames dataset \citep{culturalframes}. Image rows are joined to annotation records by exact image identifier, with prompt, country, and generator-suffix checks. We decode every image, check repeated identifiers and encoded-image content, and require at least three valid, annotated candidates. For repeated prompt text within a country we retain the lexicographically first base identifier before splitting, preventing duplicated requests from crossing splits. Missing annotations are never imputed. The provenance manifest pins the public dataset and model revisions.

\input{generated/data}

We create a development split of three prompts per country and a disjoint main split of 30 per country, using seed 20261002. Split membership depends only on a deterministic hash of prompt identifiers, without judge outputs or annotation values. The equal country allocation defines a balanced-country estimand; it does not reproduce a real deployment distribution. Remaining eligible sets form a reserve, and the main split is not expanded in response to its results.

\subsection{Open-Weight Judges}
The primary judge is Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct (hereafter Qwen) \citep{qwenmodel}; SmolVLM2-2.2B-Instruct (Smol) is the secondary judge \citep{smolvlm,smolmodel}. Model cards, revisions, dependency versions, and inference settings are stored with the artifact. We run locally with greedy decoding, a 64-token output limit, and no requested explanation. Qwen images fit an aspect-preserving 448-pixel bounding box. Smol uses its native 384-pixel global view with image tiling disabled to bound memory consumption. The two judges therefore receive different visual token budgets, so differences between them cannot be attributed to model capability alone.

Every request interleaves a candidate label with its image. The instruction asks for explicit prompt satisfaction, implicit contextual consistency, avoidance of unsupported stereotypes, and visual integrity. It warns against rewarding cultural symbols merely for their number or recognizability. Only the original prompt supplies cultural context. Source model names, category labels, country metadata, and human annotations do not enter the request.

For Qwen the output schema is a JSON object with \texttt{choice} and \texttt{ranking}. The ranking must contain every supplied label exactly once, and its first label must equal the choice. Smol receives the same evaluation criteria but a choice-only JSON contract, selected during development because it could not reliably satisfy the full-ranking format. Its adapter can normalize a single explicit label in a one-field structured object; raw JSON and exact-schema validity are tracked separately from recovered decision validity. Rankings are not used by any outcome metric. These interface differences limit comparisons of formatting reliability. We record raw text, parsed output, label-to-image mapping, input tensor shapes, model revision, elapsed time, and request hashes. Unit tests verify regret extrema, the analytic random expectation, tie behavior, annotation aggregation, and identity preservation under permutation.

\subsection{Candidate Order and Invalid Outputs}
Each prompt receives three distinct cyclic presentations: its canonical identifier order and two deterministic rotations. Canonical order sorts candidate identifiers, which usually places the same generator first; the rotations break this link, so generator effects are assessed over all three orders. The instruction, images, and decoding settings remain fixed. Thus disagreement measures sensitivity to these presentation changes rather than sampled decoding variation or prompt paraphrases. Three rotations do not exhaust all orders, especially for four candidates.

The original-order decision is the primary direct policy. No main-run prompt edits are permitted. An unresolved parse failure produces an abstention and remains in the output log. Qwen uses strict schema validation; Smol uses its predeclared single-label normalization, with every recovery flagged. A runtime exception permits one retry with identical inputs; invalid text is not repeatedly sampled until valid. Utility and regret are conditional on valid decisions, with coverage relative to the full frozen split reported alongside them. We never assign failed requests a favorable candidate or silently remove their denominator.

\subsection{Selection with Abstention}
The gate thresholds $\tau\in\{1/3,2/3,1\}$ and the cross-model rule were declared before main inference. A gate acts only when all three order calls parse; ties in the majority are broken by canonical identifier order, so the action never depends on human ratings. Abstention leaves a request unresolved: we do not assume a human fallback or a zero-risk outcome, and we report coverage next to every selective estimate. Because Smol is a weaker judge (Section~\ref{sec:results}), cross-model results are conditional on that pairing.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
The unit of analysis is a prompt with its candidate pool; the three order calls for a prompt are never resampled independently. We report 95\% intervals from 10,000 prompt-level bootstrap resamples (seed 20261002) for means and paired differences, and Wilson intervals for a single policy's BMR. Intervals condition on the released annotation means. Secondary cultural-error intervals are pointwise, and we additionally report Bonferroni-adjusted intervals across the three error types. Analyses of presentation position, of the prompts that gates reject, and all ablations in Section~\ref{sec:ablations} were added after main inference and are labeled exploratory. Appendix~\ref{app:stats} gives further estimation details.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{Evaluate the action, not the score.}
A judge that ranks images plausibly can still return a poor image when it must act. Comparing the returned image with the best available one and with random choice on the same pool makes the value of the decision visible, and it exposes failures that score correlation hides: here, a strong dependence on where an image appears.

\paragraph{Agreement gates need their own audit.}
Agreement can retain stable mistakes, and any reduction in regret must be compared with random choice on the kept prompts, and with the judge on the rejected ones. Position bias explains why the two gates we studied diverge. Unanimity across rotations demands the same image from different slots, so it filters out position-driven choices; two judges with similar slot preferences can agree for reasons unrelated to content. Order averaging, by voting across rotations, recovers part of the lost value without abstaining. Abstention still leaves a practical question: who handles the rejected request, at what cost, and with what outcome.

\paragraph{Is the unanimity result explained by easy prompts?}
No. Candidates on the prompts that unanimity keeps differ more in rating than those on the rejected prompts, so the kept pools leave more room for error, not less (Section~\ref{sec:results}); the judge's gain on them is large, and its gain on the rejected prompts is below zero.

\paragraph{Why small open-weight judges?}
Open weights allow a frozen, fully logged protocol with exact revisions and inputs. Larger judges may show weaker position bias, but the audit applies unchanged, and its main lesson does not depend on the judge: agreement rules need matched baselines and an audit of what they reject.

\paragraph{Alignment and cultural errors are different targets.}
Both the VLM judge and the CLIP baseline reduce missing cultural expectations while slightly raising stereotype ratings. A selector tuned or validated on prompt alignment should therefore be audited separately on stereotype outcomes, and composite scores would hide this trade-off.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}
CulturalFrames covers a limited set of countries, domains, prompts, and generators. Country labels compress substantial within-country diversity. Candidate images are existing outputs from a small generator pool, so the results depend on its visual styles and available alternatives. Hiding generator names does not remove recognizable stylistic signatures. Our local input resolution can obscure fine details and is part of the evaluated configuration.

Human annotation means are imperfect references. Different annotators can disagree, small rating panels create ties and noise, and selecting a maximum can exploit annotation noise. The oracle should be interpreted accordingly. Primary bootstrap intervals condition on these means; they are not estimates of uncertainty over all possible human evaluations. Prompts can share semantic templates even after exact-text deduplication, so unmodeled dependence may also narrow intervals. Prompt alignment reflects ordinary request satisfaction and cannot isolate culture-specific value by itself. Missing-expectation and stereotype endpoints provide additional descriptions without an arbitrary composite.

The setting simulates selecting a returned image; it measures neither observed user harm nor live sequential editing. We do not treat independent generations as temporal trajectories or claim a feedback-amplification result. Two small open judges (4B and 2.2B parameters) do not represent larger or proprietary models, quantizations, prompts, or compute budgets; stronger judges may show weaker position preference. With 30 prompts per country, country-group intervals are wide, and the study is underpowered to detect moderate group differences. Position, complement-set, content-baseline, and ablation analyses were added after main inference and are exploratory. The CLIP comparison uses a single similarity model. Agreement statistics are not epistemic confidence, and selective risk is not overall system risk when abstained cases remain unresolved.

This is secondary analysis of an independently released public benchmark, with no new participant recruitment or collection of personal data. Derived artifacts retain aggregate scores and candidate identifiers, not annotator identifiers, demographics, or free-text comments. The public dataset card does not declare a dataset license; public availability does not establish redistribution permission. We provide newly written code, manifests, and derived outputs; source images and raw human records are not redistributed. Figures~\ref{fig:hero}, \ref{fig:teaser}, and~\ref{fig:gallery} reproduce dataset images with attribution for illustration. Applicable institutional requirements for secondary analysis remain an author responsibility.

\paragraph{AI assistance.}
AI coding and writing assistants (OpenAI Codex and Anthropic Claude) assisted with code development, manuscript drafting and revision, figure code, and draft review. All empirical results are computed from released annotations and recorded local judge outputs; writing assistance does not supply experimental observations. Authors remain responsible for verifying the analysis, references, and final manuscript.

\paragraph{Statement of Contributions.}
This paper contributes (i) a matched-baseline, order-rotated audit protocol for VLM judges that select from fixed candidate pools, released with a deterministic artifact and an offline browser for every recorded decision (Appendix~\ref{app:browser}); (ii) evidence of strong position bias in two small open judges, with post-hoc ablations of the output format and label identity; and (iii) an audit of agreement gates on both the prompts they keep and the prompts they reject, compared with a content-only baseline. The paper has no earlier published version; all experiments, analyses, and text are new.

\section{Design Choices and Anticipated Questions}
\paragraph{Why prompt alignment as the primary outcome?} It is the rating most directly tied to whether a returned image satisfies the request, and it is available for every candidate. Cultural-error ratings are reported separately rather than folded into a composite, because the results show they can move in different directions.
\paragraph{Why not debias position directly?} Order averaging or position-balanced prompting would change the frozen policy after observing results. We report the bias and leave debiased selectors to future work, where they can be evaluated with the same audit.
\paragraph{What happens to abstained requests?} We do not model a human fallback. Coverage is reported alongside every selective estimate so that the cost of abstention remains visible.

\section{Estimation Details}
\label{app:stats}
We use 10,000 nonparametric prompt-level bootstrap resamples with seed 20261002 for means and paired gains. Each prompt's candidates and decisions travel together; permutation calls are never independently resampled. Binary deterministic-policy BMR uses Wilson intervals. Random-policy BMR averages fractional within-prompt probabilities and uses a bootstrap interval. Country results have subgroup bootstrap intervals, and the worst-group statistic is recomputed within resamples rather than selecting the upper endpoint of a single country's interval.

The pooled prompt bootstrap approximates sampling from the balanced source mixture; it does not hold country counts fixed in every replicate. Worst-group resampling is stratified within represented countries, but its percentile interval is descriptive and need not be well calibrated when group means are tied or nearly tied. An interval for the group with the largest observed mean is not simultaneous or selection-adjusted.

All intervals condition on the released annotation means. They do not account for annotation uncertainty or recruitment bias. Estimates after abstention weight accepted prompts equally, so country weights can differ from the balanced source split. We report group coverage and compare random selection on the same accepted prompts. The original endpoints, acceptance thresholds, and sensitive-set definitions remain fixed throughout the main run.

Intervals for the secondary cultural-rating outcomes are pointwise, without adjustment for multiple comparisons. We interpret them as descriptive evidence about the released ratings, not confirmatory evidence of cultural harm across populations.

\paragraph{Random-choice below-mean probability.} For random choice the below-mean event is a probability, $\frac{1}{m_p}\sum_i \mathbf{1}\{h_{pi}<\bar h_p\}$, averaged over the prompts in $A_\pi$. Replacing it with the indicator of the expected utility would make it identically zero. A sensitivity margin $\delta=0.05$ replaces $\bar h_p$ by $\bar h_p-\delta$ (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}).
\paragraph{Order-consistency entropy.} We also record the normalized entropy of the three choices, $H_p=-\sum_i f_{pi}\log f_{pi}/\log m_p$, where $f_{pi}$ is the share of orders choosing $x_{pi}$. With three calls it can reach one for three candidates but at most $\log 3/\log 4$ for four, so it is not comparable across pool sizes; it plays no role in the gates.
\paragraph{Worst-group regret.} For country group $g$, $R_g$ is the mean regret over acted-on prompts in $g$; worst-group regret is $\max_g R_g$ and disparity is $\max_g R_g-\min_g R_g$. Groups with no accepted prompts are reported as zero coverage, not zero risk.

\section{Judge Instruction}
The shared instruction reads:
\begin{quote}
You are evaluating candidate images for a generative image system. Select the ONE image that should be returned to the user.

Evaluate only evidence visible in the images and information stated or reasonably implied by the prompt. Consider explicit prompt satisfaction, implicit contextual consistency, avoidance of unsupported cultural stereotyping, and visual integrity.

Do not prefer an image merely because it contains more cultural symbols. Do not assume that stereotypical visual markers imply greater cultural accuracy.

The candidate labels are supplied next to each image. Return JSON only, with choice equal to the first element of ranking and ranking containing every candidate label exactly once. Do not explain your decision.
\end{quote}
The user prompt follows this instruction, then each label and image are interleaved. A final text segment states the allowed labels and requires the two JSON keys, without specifying a candidate as an example answer. No human annotation, generator name, country field, or category field is included.

\section{Invalid Output and Reproducibility}
Qwen's strict parser accepts exactly the two requested fields, a complete permutation of supplied labels, and a choice equal to the first ranking entry. It rejects explanatory wrappers, repeated labels, incomplete rankings, and inconsistent top choices. Smol requests exactly one \texttt{choice} key. Its adapter also accepts a bare supplied label or a one-field JSON or Python-literal dictionary using a declared alias (\texttt{image}, \texttt{selected}, \texttt{selected\_image}, or \texttt{candidate}), with a value consisting solely of a supplied label, optionally preceded by ``Candidate''. Every alias, bare label, or non-JSON literal is marked as a recovery. Multiple fields, multiple labels, absent candidates, explanatory prose, and ambiguous values are rejected. The adapter never invents a ranking or consults human scores. Its output instruction replaces the ranking request with a choice-only contract while retaining the evaluation criteria. Raw output is retained for every attempt. Exceptions receive one identical retry; unresolved parse failures produce abstentions. Unique request keys combine prompt identity and permutation index, and resume checks require unchanged instruction and manifest hashes.

All public source revisions, model cards, candidate identities, frozen orders, environment versions, and derived tables are saved. Exact source-image bytes are retained locally; aspect-preserving resizing occurs only at inference. The pipeline accesses only public sources. Floating-point greedy decoding is deterministic within the recorded runtime configuration but need not yield identical choices across hardware or library implementations.
